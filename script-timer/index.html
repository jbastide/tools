How long should a short-form script be?

Spoken runtime is a function of two things: how many words you wrote, and how fast you say them. The formula this tool uses is simply seconds = (words ÷ WPM) × 60, with optional pause weighting added for punctuation. Everything else — zones, warnings, colour — is that number sliced against the moments where short-form audiences actually decide to leave.

The mechanics of the 3-second hook

Short-form feeds are built around an immediate swipe decision. The viewer has already committed to scrolling before your video loads, so the opening line is not an introduction — it is an interruption. Whatever makes the video worth watching has to be legible inside the first breath.

Three seconds is a working budget, not a law of physics, but it is a useful constraint because it is brutally short: at a normal storytime cadence it buys you about eight words. That is one clause. If your opening line needs a subordinate clause to land, it is too long.

What tends to burn hook time

  • Throat-clearing. "Hey guys, so today I wanted to talk about…" is three seconds of nothing.
  • Context before conflict. Setup belongs in zone 2, after the viewer has a reason to care.
  • Long proper nouns and numbers. They read fast on the page and speak slowly out loud.
  • Qualifiers. "kind of", "basically", "I think" — each one costs you a fifth of a second.

The rule this tool enforces: if your first sentence runs past 3.5 seconds, you get a warning. That half-second of slack exists because a hook that lands at 3.2s and cuts hard is usually fine; one that rambles to 5s is not.

The first four bands are conventional; the last is measured. We timed the first 30 seconds of 132 YouTube Shorts with at least a million views each and found a median of 193 WPM — so what this table calls a very fast cadence is the ordinary pace of a top-performing Short, not an outlier.

Everyday conversation sits somewhere in the 120–150 range for most English speakers; scripted short-form voiceover usually runs faster than that because the pauses get edited out. If your recorded runtime consistently comes in longer than this timer predicts, lower the WPM until the estimate matches your real reads — that number is your personal cadence, and it is worth knowing.

Frequently asked questions

How many words is a 30-second video script?

About 78 words at a standard 155 WPM storytime pace. A deliberate 135 WPM read fits 68 words; a fast 195 WPM read fits 98. Budget 10–15% fewer if you pause for emphasis or cut to B-roll.

How many words is a 60-second video script?

155 words at 155 WPM — the number of words and the words-per-minute rate are the same at exactly one minute. The full range across normal speaking rates is 135 to 195 words.

How many words is a 15-second video script?

Roughly 39 words at 155 WPM, or 34 to 49 across the full range of speaking rates. At that length there is room for a hook, one point, and a close — nothing else.

How many words should a 3-second hook be?

7 to 10 words, depending on your pace — 8 at a standard 155 WPM. That is one clause. Measured across 132 top-performing Shorts, a median of 9 words land before the 3-second mark, so the rule holds; their first sentence runs a median of 11 words and about 3 seconds, with half taking longer. This timer treats your first sentence as the hook and warns you when it runs past 3.5 seconds.

What speaking rate should I use for TikTok, Reels, and Shorts?

Measured across 132 top-performing Shorts, the median is 193 WPM and the middle half runs 156 to 221 — faster than everyday conversation because the pauses get edited out. Start with the 155 WPM preset if you read deliberately, or the 195 WPM Top Shorts preset to match what tends to perform; if your recorded videos consistently run longer than the estimate, drop the WPM until it matches your real reads.

How does the script timer calculate duration?

It counts words, divides by your words-per-minute rate, and multiplies by 60. With Smart pauses on it also adds 0.35 seconds per sentence end, 0.15 per comma or dash, and 0.5 per paragraph break. Each word is then colour-coded by the moment it finishes — the 3-second, 15-second, 30-second, and 60-second retention zones.

Is my script saved or uploaded anywhere?

No. The draft is stored in your browser's local storage so it survives a refresh, and it never leaves your device. The page makes no network requests after it loads.

How the timer calculates

  • Word counting uses /\b\w+(?:['’]\w+)?\b/g, so contractions count once and double spaces are ignored. Hyphenated compounds count as two words, which matches how they are spoken.
  • Base duration is (words ÷ WPM) × 60. With smart pauses off, 155 words at 155 WPM is exactly 1:00.0.
  • Smart pauses add +0.35s per sentence-ending run (. ! ?), +0.15s per internal break (, ; : —), and +0.50s per paragraph break.
  • Zone colour is assigned per word, based on the cumulative time at which that word finishes.
  • Everything runs in your browser. Your draft is saved to this device's local storage and never leaves it. There is no account, no upload, and no server.
