Lead generation for lawyers: what works, what it costs, and where leads get lost

Lead generation for lawyers is everything a law firm does to get people with a legal problem to contact it: referrals, search, ads and bought leads. Attorney lead generation is sold in many forms, and law firm owners report very different results from each. This guide compares the channels using 188 coded r/LawFirm threads and the prices owners said they paid, and shows how to judge any of them by cost per signed case.

By Copy for Originals · Published October 13, 2026 · Last updated October 13, 2026

Educational only, not legal advice. This page reports what law firm owners wrote in public threads and quotes public rules. Copy for Originals manages Google Ads and does not sell leads. Your state bar’s rules and your own counsel decide what your firm may do.

What is lead generation for lawyers?

Lead generation for lawyers is the work of getting people who need a lawyer to call, write or fill in a form. A law firm has six main sources: referrals, organic search and its Google Business Profile, Google Search ads, Local Services Ads, social media ads, and leads bought from a vendor. Each is paid for differently and fails differently.

Which lead generation channels work for law firms?

No channel worked for every firm in our data, and the complaints differ by channel. Owners who paid per lead mostly described junk leads: 15 of 20 threads about lead vendors and 13 of 19 about Local Services Ads. Owners who paid for SEO mostly described getting no return: 17 of 39 threads.

The pattern makes sense. A firm that pays per lead is charged for every inquiry, including the ones it cannot use. A firm that pays a monthly SEO fee is charged whether or not anything arrives.

Owners also reported channels working. A family law and probate firm said its leads went from about 450 a month to between 850 and 900 after it raised its SEO spend to between $7,500 and $10,000 a month. A solo lawyer said Facebook lead ads aimed at Spanish speakers grew into $500,000 of revenue over four years. A family law solo spending $10,450 a month on Google Ads said 80% of clients came from it. Another owner spent $50,000 to $60,000 on a website and SEO over about two and a half years and called it wasted.

A survey gives a wider view. In CallRail’s October 2025 survey of 100 legal professionals, firms named these channels as driving new business: SEO 54%, video 50%, paid search 47%, paid social 38% and content 37%. In that survey, 77% of respondents worked at firms with 100 or more employees.

How much does a law firm lead cost?

Law firm owners on r/LawFirm reported Local Services Ads leads from about $30 (family law) to $300 (personal injury and criminal defense), Google Search ads leads from $80 to $1,000, and bought leads from $40 to $150. SEO is paid by the month, and 14 owners reported a median of $3,000.

Should a law firm buy lawyer leads?

Treat bought lawyer leads as a test and judge them by signed cases. In the 20 threads where a law firm owner described a lead vendor, 15 described junk leads, 7 said they paid and got no cases or no return, and 3 said the leads had also been sold to other firms. A few owners said bought leads paid off.

The owners who were satisfied had a way to work the leads and a count of what they produced. A criminal defense owner paying $50 a lead for about 40 leads a month called it successful once a follow-up system was in place. A bankruptcy lawyer converted 6 of 20 leads at $150 each and still found the price too high for the budget. A family law solo traced $693.98 of marketplace leads to four clients and $8,000 in fees, then stopped when later spending returned less. An employment lawyer paid a lawyer referral service $1,000 to $2,000 a month and said it supplied early clients.

The ABA’s model rules allow paying for leads with conditions. Comment 5 to Model Rule 7.2 says: “A lawyer may pay others for generating client leads, such as Internet-based client leads, as long as the lead generator does not recommend the lawyer, any payment to the lead generator is consistent with Rules 1.5(e) (division of fees) and 5.4 (professional independence of the lawyer), and the lead generator’s communications are consistent with Rule 7.1” on communications about a lawyer’s services.

The personal injury lawyer leads guide has six questions to ask a lead vendor, five injury owners’ results from bought leads, and one firm’s cost per signed case month by month. The questions apply to any practice area.

How much should a law firm spend on lead generation?

There is no single right number. Owners in the threads spent from a few hundred dollars a month to $50,000 or more, and what mattered was cost per signed case, not the total. For Google Search ads the minimum monthly budget is three times the expected cost per lead.

How do you measure law firm lead generation?

Measure law firm lead generation by cost per signed case for each source: what the firm spent on that source, including fees, divided by the cases it signed from it. Leads, calls and clicks are steps along the way and can rise while signed cases fall.

The formula is cost per signed case = (spend on the source + fees) ÷ signed cases from that source. To compare sources before any cases are signed, use cost per lead ÷ the share of leads that are real potential clients ÷ the share of those that sign.

One family law solo posted the figures month by month: $4,000 of Google Ads in February against $14,018 collected, $5,302 in March against $37,343, and $7,500 in April against $32,810. Those are the firm’s total collections for the month, not only money from ad clients, but tracking the two side by side is what let the owner decide to keep raising spend.

Many firms cannot do this. In 9 of the 188 threads the owner said they could not tell which leads came from where. How does a law firm track signed cases back to Google Ads clicks? gives the setup for search ads, and the Local Services Ads guide has a calculator that compares two sources.

Why do law firm leads not become clients?

Law firm leads are lost mostly at intake: the call is not answered, nobody calls back, or the person never had a case the firm takes. In the secret-shopper test in Clio’s 2024 Legal Trends Report, 33% of firms responded to an email and 48% neither answered the phone nor called back.

Three more findings point the same way:

  • In CallRail’s December 2024 survey of 49 respondents, 96% of law firms said a slow response or no response can lead to lost cases. CallRail’s own call data showed 28% of calls to law firms were missed.
  • In 207 of the lowest-rated Google reviews at 11 Chicago personal injury firms, 38% describe no callback or an unreachable lawyer.
  • In 12 of the 188 r/LawFirm threads, the owner said the firm’s own intake was the problem.

Some loss is not intake’s fault. The family and probate firm whose leads doubled said that even after improvement, 48% of its leads were disqualified. An employment lawyer getting five to seven leads a day said most callers had no viable case. Count disqualified leads separately from unanswered ones, because the fixes are different: better targeting for the first, a staffed phone for the second.

Pay-per-lead channels add a cost to slow answers. Google charges for a Local Services Ads call missed during business hours once the caller has waited more than 20 seconds.

What rules apply to lawyer lead generation?

Three ABA Model Rules cover most lawyer lead generation: Rule 7.1 bars false or misleading communications about a lawyer’s services, Rule 7.2 limits paying others to recommend a lawyer, and Rule 7.3 restricts soliciting people directly. Each state adopts its own version, so check yours.

  • Rule 7.1. “A lawyer shall not make a false or misleading communication about the lawyer or the lawyer’s services.” It applies to ads, landing pages and anything a lead vendor says on the firm’s behalf.
  • Rule 7.2(b). “A lawyer shall not compensate, give or promise anything of value to a person for recommending the lawyer’s services,” with exceptions that include paying the reasonable costs of advertising. Comment 5, quoted above, covers lead vendors.
  • Rule 7.3. Restricts solicitation. Our Chicago study checked Illinois’s version: it bars real-time contact the lawyer starts, while a chat or text the person starts is a response to a request.

Two narrower questions have their own pages. Can a law firm bid on a competitor’s name in Google Ads? lists seven states’ positions, and Does Google record calls to law firms from its ads? covers recorded intake calls and confidentiality.

In our thread data, 6 of the 188 threads mentioned a vendor tactic that might break a rule, and 4 of those 6 were about lead vendors.

What can a law firm do before paying for leads?

Fix what happens after a lead arrives, and do the free work first. In 45 of the 188 threads, owners said doing marketing themselves or in-house worked better than paying a vendor, mostly for content and SEO. Our Chicago study lists eight actions that need no new ad spend.

  • Answer and return every call. Unanswered calls show up in every data set on this page.
  • Make the Google Business Profile true. In Chicago, 74 of 100 injury firms told Google they were open 24 hours, and only about half of their websites said the same.
  • Ask for reviews. Chicago firms that advertised had a median of 237 Google reviews, against 95 for firms that did not. The study notes that Illinois’s rules bar paying for reviews, not asking for them.
  • Publish what you know. One solo lawyer said a blog costing $340 a year in hosting made him known in his niche. Another spent $2,000 setting up a Google Business Profile, ads and a website himself over six months and said spending more now brings more cases.
  • Keep referrals alive. One personal injury owner opened more than 60 files in a month on referrals alone.

The eight Chicago actions, each checked against Illinois’s rules, are in the Chicago personal injury lead generation study. If the firm does hire an agency, the law firm Google Ads agency scorecard gives 12 checks to run on it.

Limitations

  • The thread counts and owner reports come from public r/LawFirm threads from October 2023 to October 2026. They are what individual owners wrote, not a sample of law firms, and unhappy owners post more.
  • Each thread is counted under one main channel. Our Local Services Ads guide counts every thread where a lawyer described their own Local Services Ads, which is a wider set of 37.
  • Most of the individual examples rest on one owner each, and vendor names are withheld.
  • Social media ads, video and television are barely covered; we have little data on them.
  • CallRail’s surveys are small and lean toward larger firms. The Chicago figures are for one city.
  • The ABA Model Rule text was read from archived copies of the ABA’s pages. We could not open Rule 7.3 and describe it through Illinois’s version. No state’s rules are covered.
  • Not legal advice.
