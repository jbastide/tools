Google Ads for law firms: what it costs, what goes wrong, and how to tell if it’s working

Google Ads for law firms is paid search advertising that shows a firm’s ad when someone searches Google for a lawyer, and charges the firm for each click. This guide covers what it costs, the five problems law firms run into, and how to tell whether a campaign is working. It is written for lawyers, attorneys and law firm owners who run Google Ads (formerly Google AdWords) themselves or pay an agency to run it.

By Copy for Originals · Published October 13, 2026 · Last updated October 13, 2026

Educational only, not legal advice. This page describes Google Ads practice and points to public rules, opinions and cases. Your state bar’s rules and your own counsel decide what your firm may do.

Does Google Ads work for law firms?

Google Ads works for a law firm when three things are true: the budget buys enough clicks to judge the campaign, the firm counts signed cases instead of leads, and someone answers the phone. When one of those is missing, law firm owners report paying for clicks and getting no cases.

In 312 r/LawFirm threads from October 2023 to October 2026, 188 had law firm owners describing their own paid marketing. Of those 188 threads, 63 (34%) said the owner paid and got no cases or no return, 57 (30%) described junk leads, 37 (20%) said the market was too expensive for the budget, and 45 (24%) said doing it themselves or in-house worked better. The counts and the coded threads are in the law firm Google Ads agency scorecard.

The same three conditions apply whether the firm calls it Google Ads, Google AdWords, Google PPC or pay-per-click advertising for lawyers. They are the same product: Search ads bought by the click.

How much does Google Ads cost a law firm?

Google Ads costs a law firm its own cost per click divided by its conversion rate for each lead, and the monthly budget should be at least three times that cost per lead. At $100 a click and a 5% conversion rate, a lead costs $2,000 and the minimum budget is $6,000 a month.

LocaliQ’s 2026 Search Advertising Benchmarks put the average Google Ads click for Attorneys & Legal Services at $9.87, against $5.42 across all niches. That average mixes every keyword and every city. The keywords that bring cases in a large city cost far more, so plan with the cost per click in your own account, or the top of page bid (high range) in Google’s Keyword Planner.

Agency management is a separate cost: four owners reported a median of $1,175 a month for Google Ads management, with ad spend on top. Those figures are in How much do legal marketing agencies charge?

Should a law firm use Local Services Ads or Google Search ads?

Local Services Ads charge a law firm for each lead and Google Search ads charge for each click, so compare the two by cost per signed case, not cost per lead. Cost per signed case = cost per lead ÷ share of leads that are real potential clients ÷ sign rate.

A cheap lead is not a cheap case if most of the leads are the wrong practice area or can’t be reached. In 37 r/LawFirm threads where lawyers described their own Local Services Ads, 10 reported paying for junk leads.

Change under way, checked October 13, 2026. Google is phasing out the separate Local Services Ads platform. Existing campaigns are being moved automatically into Google Ads as “Performance Max campaigns with pay-per-lead goals.” Google’s schedule puts professional services in late 2026 on one page and 2027 on another, and does not name lawyers. Firms still pay per valid lead, and the ads still show only on Google Search and Maps. See what changes for law firms.

The Local Services Ads vs Google Ads guide for lawyers covers what changes, which leads Google credits, and a calculator that compares the two on your own numbers.

What goes wrong with Google Ads for law firms?

Five problems account for most of what goes wrong with Google Ads for law firms: clicks that cost more than the budget allows, disputes over bidding on another firm’s name, recorded calls, searches from people who will never hire a lawyer, and leads lost at intake.

Is Google Ads worth it for a law firm?

Google Ads is worth it for a law firm when the cost per signed case is lower than what the firm can afford to pay for a case, and a firm can only know that by counting signed cases. The widely repeated figure that 82% of law firms say Google Ads isn’t worth it comes from a December 2021 survey and is narrower than it sounds.

The 82% figure comes from CallRail’s “2022 Marketing Outlook for Law Firms,” a survey of 508 US law firm employees conducted in December 2021. Among law firms that ran paid search, 82% agreed with the statement “Paid search marketing is too expensive to get good ROI.” The question was about expense. It did not ask about tracking, lead quality or signed retainers.

The sample was not small firms. Almost 65% of respondents worked at law firms with 250 to 999 employees, 3% worked at firms with 1 to 19, and the most common job title was IT manager (26%), followed by law firm partner (16%). CallRail sells call tracking software.

The same report found that 78% of law firms whose clients find them through search ran paid search. The two numbers are often quoted together as “78% of law firms use paid search and 82% don’t think it works,” which drops both conditions.

CallRail’s later reports give different numbers for the same statement: 97% in its October 2022 survey and 25% in its December 2024 survey of 49 respondents. The surveys side by side, and how the figure changed as it was repeated, are in Do 82% of law firms say Google Ads isn’t worth it?

Can a law firm bid on a competitor’s name in Google Ads?

Whether a law firm can bid on a competitor’s name in Google Ads depends on the state. Ethics opinions in North Carolina, Ohio and Michigan say no, those in Texas and South Carolina say yes with conditions, and the New Jersey Supreme Court allows it with a disclaimer on the landing page.

Bidding on a competing lawyer’s name as a Google Ads keyword: positions in seven states
StateAuthorityPosition
North CarolinaState Bar, 2010 Formal Ethics Opinion 14 (adopted April 27, 2012)Not allowed: “it is a violation of Rule 8.4(c) for a lawyer to select another lawyer’s name to be used in his own keyword advertising.”
OhioBoard of Professional Conduct, Opinion 2021-04 (June 11, 2021; nonbinding advisory opinion)Not allowed. “A lawyer or law firm may not purchase the name of another lawyer or law firm for use in competitive keyword online advertising.”
MichiganState Bar of Michigan, Opinion RI-385 (November 18, 2022)Not allowed without the other lawyer’s or firm’s express consent.
TexasProfessional Ethics Committee, Opinion 661 (July 2016)Allowed. The ad must not be false or misleading and must not suggest the lawyers are affiliated.
South CarolinaBar Ethics Advisory Committee, Opinion 20-01Allowed. No derogatory or uncivil message, and no link that looks like the competitor’s site and redirects to the lawyer’s own.
New JerseySupreme Court, In re Opinion No. 735 (decided May 22, 2025)Allowed. The landing page must carry a set disclaimer saying the visitor arrived through a paid advertisement.
FloridaRule 4-7.13(b)(12), Rules Regulating The Florida Bar (effective May 2, 2022)Depends on the ad. It must not mislead a searcher into contacting a different lawyer.

Other states may have their own opinions, or none. Check your own state bar’s position before bidding on another firm’s name. The full guide quotes each opinion and covers what to do when the name is yours: Can a law firm bid on a competitor’s name in Google Ads?

Trademark law is a separate question, and federal appeals courts have come out differently on different facts. In Lerner & Rowe v. Brown Engstrand & Shely (Ninth Circuit, October 22, 2024), the court held that a competitor buying “Lerner & Rowe” as a keyword was not likely to confuse consumers, and treated 236 calls that mentioned the Lerner & Rowe name as too few to matter. In Adler v. McNeil Consultants (Fifth Circuit, August 10, 2021), the court let a claim go forward on allegations that a referral service bought a lawyer’s name and nicknames as keywords, ran generic click-to-call ads, and kept confused callers on the line.

In Colorado, Franklin D. Azar & Associates sued an Alabama law firm in 2022 over Google ads that Azar said made callers believe they had reached his firm. The Alabama firm agreed to pay $1.55 million to settle, BusinessDen reported on December 12, 2024.

In Adler and in the Azar suit, the complaint was about ads and call handling that left callers thinking they had reached the other firm, not about the keyword purchase alone. That is our reading of the reports, not a legal rule.

Does Google record calls to a law firm from its ads?

Yes, in the United States and Canada. Google’s Local Services Ads help page says all inbound calls through the Google forwarding number are recorded by Google, and its Google Ads help page says the call recording setting is now enabled by default. Callers hear an automated notice first.

Google’s Local Services Ads help page reads: “in the US and Canada, all inbound calls from customers via the Google forwarding number are recorded by Google to improve quality and support policies and research (and customers are notified of this by a pre-recorded message immediately before you hear the ‘Call from Google’ notification).”

For Search campaigns, Google’s help page on call recording reads: “The call recording setting is now enabled by default to allow for AI-powered lead qualification.” It applies to calls that go through a Google forwarding number from call ads, call assets or website visits. Recording stays off for advertisers who turned it off earlier and for businesses Google identified as healthcare or financial services. That page does not name legal services as a sensitive industry. The setting is in Google Ads under Admin, Account settings, Call ads, Call recording.

We found no state ethics opinion that says Local Services Ads breach a lawyer’s duty of confidentiality. North Carolina’s ethics committee proposed one in 2021 (Proposed 2021 Formal Ethics Opinion 5) that would have barred lawyers from the program, and withdrew it in April 2022 after concluding the recordings “are not uniquely vulnerable to harmful third-party disclosures.” The State Bar of Wisconsin’s ethics counsel took the other view in a January 2021 article: without the caller’s informed consent, a lawyer who authorizes the recording “violates the duty of confidentiality.” That article is commentary, not a formal opinion.

The North Carolina State Bar Journal article on the withdrawal still reminds lawyers of their duty to make “reasonable investigations into any advertising program” before joining it, and says callers should be warned that a call will not create a client-lawyer relationship and that what they say will not be confidential or privileged. Whether a recorded intake call affects privilege in your state is a question for your own counsel.

Google keeps Local Services recordings in legal categories for 60 days. Retention, who can listen, what callers hear and how to turn recording off are in Does Google record calls to law firms from its ads?

Which searches waste a law firm’s Google Ads budget?

Searches from people who are not looking to hire a lawyer waste a law firm’s Google Ads budget: job seekers, students, people who want free forms, and people with a legal problem the firm doesn’t handle. Negative keywords block those searches, and phrase and exact match keywords keep Google from matching the ad to loose ones.

The five short answers for law firm Search campaigns list starter negative keywords by type of searcher and explain when broad match is safe to use. The Negative Keyword Finder reads a search terms report in the browser and lists the terms that spent money without converting.

Why don’t Google Ads leads turn into signed cases?

Google Ads leads fail to become signed cases mostly after the click: the call isn’t answered, nobody calls back, or the caller was never a potential client. A firm that only counts leads can’t see which of the three it has.

In the secret-shopper test in Clio’s 2024 Legal Trends Report, 33% of law firms responded to an email inquiry, and 48% neither answered the phone nor called back, as summarized by the Illinois Supreme Court Commission on Professionalism.

In 207 of the lowest-rated Google reviews at 11 Chicago personal injury firms, 38% describe no callback or an unreachable lawyer, and 20% describe a case declined or dropped after intake. Of 42 Chicago personal injury advertisers, 20 show no call tracking on their homepage. Both counts are from the Chicago personal injury lead generation study.

Google Ads can only bid toward signed cases if the firm sends them back. Importing signed cases as offline conversions, using only the click ID and a value, tells Smart Bidding which searches bring clients and keeps what the caller said out of Google Ads. How does a law firm track signed cases back to Google Ads clicks? gives the setup steps, the 90-day upload limit and what each method sends to Google.

How can a law firm tell if its Google Ads is working?

A law firm’s Google Ads is working when its cost per signed case is below what the firm can afford to pay for a case. Cost per signed case = (ad spend + agency fee) ÷ signed cases.

Zero leads is a verdict only after enough clicks. After 3 ÷ conversion rate clicks with no conversion (120 clicks at a 2.5% conversion rate), the chance that the campaign is just unlucky is under 5%. The PPC Gumball Rule has the table by conversion rate and a calculator, and the PPC Troubleshooter runs that math and lists likely causes. Few advertisers do this: in our study of 62 forum threads asking why Google Ads got clicks but no conversions, only 3 posters stated a target CPA or expected conversion rate, and those who gave a click count had a median of 190 clicks before asking.

A firm that pays an agency can check the agency from inside its own account. The law firm Google Ads agency scorecard has 12 checks, and three are must-haves: Admin access to the account, the Google Ads customer ID, and the exact amount Google charged reported separately from the agency’s fee.
