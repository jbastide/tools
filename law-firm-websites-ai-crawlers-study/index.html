Do law firm websites block AI crawlers?

Copy for Originals checked the websites of 100 Chicago personal injury law firms on October 13, 2026. Only 3 disallow an AI crawler in robots.txt. But 15 of the 78 sites that could be tested refused a request carrying the name GPTBot, ClaudeBot or PerplexityBot, and 14 of those 15 have no robots.txt rule against the crawler they refused.

By Copy for Originals · Published October 13, 2026 · Last updated October 13, 2026 · Download the data (CSV)

Educational only, not legal advice. This study describes public files and server responses. Whether a firm should let AI crawlers read its site is the firm’s own decision.

What did the study find?

  1. Almost no firm blocks AI crawlers in robots.txt. 11 of 100 robots.txt files name an AI crawler at all, and 3 disallow one. Only 1 disallows both GPTBot and ClaudeBot.
  2. The refusals happen at the server, where robots.txt doesn’t show them. 15 of 78 sites answered a browser-style request but refused a request carrying the name GPTBot, ClaudeBot or PerplexityBot every time. 14 of the 15 have no robots.txt rule against the crawler they refused.
  3. ClaudeBot’s name was refused most often. 11 sites refused ClaudeBot, 9 refused GPTBot and 2 refused PerplexityBot.
  4. llms.txt is common, and often automatic. 33 of 75 sites publish one. 15 of the 33 say which plugin wrote the file, and 11 of those say Yoast SEO. Another 8 sites answer the llms.txt address with robots.txt-style rules that the llms.txt proposal doesn’t define.

How many law firm websites block AI crawlers in robots.txt?

3 of 100. Each of the 100 sites has a robots.txt file, and 89 of them don’t mention an AI crawler. Of the 11 that do, 8 name crawlers without disallowing any of them.

“Disallowing” means the group of rules that names the crawler contains Disallow: / with no Allow: /. No site disallowed every crawler with a wildcard rule.

The three firms that disallow an AI crawler did it three different ways:

  • One disallows 12 of the 15 AI crawler names the study checked, including GPTBot, OAI-SearchBot, ChatGPT-User, ClaudeBot and PerplexityBot. The file gives the reason in a comment: “Disallow AI training crawlers (added 2026-04-24 to stop CPU saturation).”
  • One disallows ClaudeBot and seven other names (anthropic-ai, Google-Extended, Applebot-Extended, CCBot, Bytespider, meta-externalagent and Amazonbot) but has no rule for GPTBot.
  • One disallows GPTBot and meta-externalagent and lets ClaudeBot and OAI-SearchBot in under ordinary rules.

Two of the 80 files read live carry a Content-Signal line, the format Cloudflare published on September 24, 2025. One says search=yes, ai-input=yes, ai-train=no. The other says search=yes, ai-input=yes, ai-train=yes.

Do law firm websites block AI crawlers at the server instead?

Yes, more often than in robots.txt. 15 of 78 sites (19%) served the homepage to a browser-style request but refused the same request when it carried an AI crawler’s name. Each site was asked three times per name, in a different order each time, and “refused” here means refused all three times.

55 of the 78 sites served all three names every time. 15 refused at least one name every time. The other 8 gave mixed answers, a 429 “Too Many Requests” in one or two runs and a normal page in the others.

The refusals were 403 “Forbidden” responses at 11 sites and 429 “Too Many Requests” at 4. 7 of the 15 sites sit behind Cloudflare.

Does robots.txt show these server blocks?

No. Only 1 of the 15 sites has a robots.txt rule against a crawler it refused. 11 have no AI crawler rule at all. The remaining 3 name the refused crawler in robots.txt without disallowing it, so the file reads as an invitation and the server turns the visitor away.

It also runs the other way. Two sites disallow ClaudeBot in robots.txt and served the ClaudeBot-named request every time. RFC 9309, the robots.txt standard, says its rules “are not a form of access authorization.” The file states what the site asks of a crawler, and the crawler decides whether to comply.

What does a refusal mean for the real crawler?

It doesn’t prove the real crawler is refused. The study’s requests came from an ordinary internet connection, not from OpenAI’s, Anthropic’s or Perplexity’s own addresses. Some firewalls check that a visitor claiming to be a crawler comes from the crawler’s published addresses and refuse it if not. One site in the study answered with a page that read “Checking search engine crawler,” which is that kind of check. A refusal in this table means the site treats the crawler’s name differently from a browser. Only the site’s own server logs show what the real crawler receives.

What about the 22 sites left out of this test?

21 sites refused the browser-style request itself, and 1 couldn’t be reached, so there was nothing to compare against. The requests came from Sweden, and some of these sites may refuse visitors from outside the United States.

16 of those 21 served the page when the same request carried the name GPTBot. 11 of the 16 also served ClaudeBot and PerplexityBot. The other 5 served GPTBot only. On these sites the crawler’s name alone was enough to get past a block that stopped an ordinary request.

How many law firm websites have an llms.txt file?

33 of 75, or 44%. An llms.txt file is a markdown file at the root of a site, proposed on September 3, 2024 to help AI tools use the site.

25 of the 100 sites didn’t give a readable answer for this address and are left out.

Who wrote the files. 15 of the 33 files name the software that generated them: Yoast SEO in 11, Rank Math in 2, Hostinger Tools in 1 and MileMark Schema Pro in 1. Yoast says its file is “generated and refreshed automatically” once the feature is switched on. The other 18 files don’t say how they were made.

How big they are. The median file is about 7.7 KB. The smallest is 1.3 KB. Seven are over 50 KB, and the largest was still going at 400 KB, where the study stopped reading.

The robots.txt-style files. 8 sites answer the llms.txt address with lines such as User-agent: * and Allow: /. Six of them serve the identical 65-byte file, which includes the line Disallow-Training: /. The llms.txt proposal describes a markdown file with a title and links. It doesn’t define access rules, so nothing in the proposal says a crawler should act on these lines.

How can a law firm check whether its own website blocks AI crawlers?

  1. Read your robots.txt. Open yourfirm.com/robots.txt and search for GPTBot, OAI-SearchBot, ClaudeBot, Claude-SearchBot and PerplexityBot. A User-agent line naming one of them, followed by Disallow: /, asks that crawler to stay out.
  2. Ask about the firewall. Ask whoever runs your hosting, your Cloudflare account or your security plugin whether a bot rule refuses AI crawlers. 14 of the 15 refusals in this study don’t appear in robots.txt.
  3. Ask for the server logs. Ask for log lines containing those crawler names and the status code each one got. A 200 means the crawler received the page. A 403 or 429 means it didn’t. This is the only check that sees the real crawler.
  4. Decide training and search separately. OpenAI says GPTBot crawls content that may be used to train its models and OAI-SearchBot surfaces websites in ChatGPT’s search features. Anthropic says ClaudeBot collects content that could contribute to training and Claude-SearchBot improves search results. A firm can refuse one and allow the other.
  5. Open your llms.txt. If yourfirm.com/llms.txt exists, it should be markdown that starts with a # title and lists your pages. If it starts with User-agent, it isn’t in the format the proposal describes.

For what answer engines do with a page once they can read it, see how to write content that LLMs retrieve and cite. The firms in this study come from the Chicago personal injury lead generation study.

How was the study done?

  1. Sample: the 100 websites listed for Chicago personal injury firms in version 4 of the Copy for Originals Chicago study.
  2. robots.txt and llms.txt: each file was requested once on October 13, 2026 with a Chrome-style user agent. 80 robots.txt files were read live. The other 20 sites refused the request, so their robots.txt was read from the most recent Internet Archive copy, captured between January 13, 2024 and May 7, 2026. None of those 20 names an AI crawler.
  3. Reading the rules: rules were grouped by User-agent line as RFC 9309 describes. A crawler counts as disallowed when a group naming it contains Disallow: / or Disallow: /* and no Allow: /. Fifteen AI crawler names were checked; the table shows nine.
  4. Server test: each homepage was requested with a Chrome-style user agent and with user agents containing GPTBot/1.3, ClaudeBot/1.0 and PerplexityBot/1.0, in three runs with a different order each time. A site counts as testable when the browser-style request returned 200 in all three runs (78 sites). A name counts as refused when it got a 403 or 429 in all three runs.
  5. llms.txt: a 200 response counts as a file when it isn’t an HTML page. Files starting with User-agent were counted separately. Reading stopped at 400 KB.

Every site and every response code is in the data file.

Limitations

  • The test request is not the real crawler. It carried the crawler’s name from an ordinary connection. A site that verifies crawler addresses may refuse the test and still serve the real crawler.
  • Requests came from Sweden. 21 sites refused the browser-style request and could not be tested at the server. Results from a US connection may differ.
  • One city, one practice area, one day. 100 Chicago personal injury firms on October 13, 2026. Treat percentages as directional.
  • 20 robots.txt files are archive copies, some more than a year old. Those sites may have changed their rules since.
  • Homepage only. A site may treat inner pages differently.
  • Who set the rule is unknown. A refusal may come from the firm, its agency, its hosting company or a default setting nobody chose.
  • Not legal advice. The study says nothing about whether a firm should allow or refuse AI crawlers.

FAQ

Do law firm websites block AI crawlers like GPTBot and ClaudeBot?

Few do it in robots.txt, and more do it at the server. In a Copy for Originals study of 100 Chicago personal injury law firm websites on October 13, 2026, 3 disallowed at least one AI crawler in robots.txt. Of the 78 sites that could be tested at the server, 15 refused a request carrying the name GPTBot, ClaudeBot or PerplexityBot in all three runs: 11 refused ClaudeBot, 9 refused GPTBot and 2 refused PerplexityBot.

Can a website block AI crawlers without saying so in robots.txt?

Yes. A firewall, a hosting company or a security plugin can refuse a crawler before the website itself answers, and robots.txt does not change. In the Copy for Originals study, 14 of the 15 law firm websites that refused a request carrying an AI crawler’s name had no robots.txt rule against that crawler, and 3 of them named the refused crawler in robots.txt without disallowing it.

How many law firm websites have an llms.txt file?

33 of 75, or 44%, in the Copy for Originals study of Chicago personal injury law firm websites on October 13, 2026. Another 8 of the 75 answered the llms.txt address with a file written in robots.txt syntax, which the llms.txt proposal does not define, and 34 had no file. Of the 33 files, 11 said they were generated by Yoast SEO.
