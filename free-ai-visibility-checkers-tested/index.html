Copy for Originals · Agent-run test · AI visibility trackers, part 1

Can a free AI visibility checker tell you whether AI recommends your business?

A free AI visibility checker is a web tool that asks AI assistants such as ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude and Google’s AI features about a business category, or looks up stored answers, and reports whether your brand was named. We had an AI agent run five free, no-signup checkers on 10 Chicago personal injury law firms on two days in October 2026. Three gave results. One firm’s score jumped from 0 to 33 from one day to the next. One checker said 0% for every firm while also saying it had tested 0 prompts. And the only one of our firms that an AI agent’s own research recommended was a firm every checker scored 0.

Are free AI visibility checkers accurate?

Not as a single score, in this test. An AI agent ran five free, no-signup AI visibility checkers on 10 Chicago personal injury law firms on October 22 and 23, 2026, and only three returned results. Geoptie’s score for the same firm moved by up to 33 points from one day to the next. Loamly reported 0% for every firm while also stating that it had tested 0 prompts. Semrush and Geoptie disagreed about 2 of the 6 firms both checked. The lists of firms a checker shows are more useful than its score.

  1. Repeat runs moved. Geoptie changed its score for 4 of 10 firms between October 22 and 23. Anesi Ozmon went from 0 to 33 and Malman Law from 33 to 60. Of the firm names in Geoptie’s answer lists for the same firm and engine, 39% appeared on both days.
  2. A verdict without data. All 11 Loamly reports said 0% and “not being recommended.” All 11 also said “0 prompts tested” and “0 AI answers captured.”
  3. One engine was always empty. Geoptie’s Google engine showed “0 brands in answer” and “No results available” in all 20 runs.
  4. The banner contradicted the result. In 4 of the 7 Geoptie runs where an engine named the firm, the banner still read “You’re invisible.”
  5. Checkers disagreed with each other. Semrush gave Disparti Law Group a score of 33 with 93 mentions. Geoptie gave Disparti 0 on both days, yet named Disparti 10 times in answers shown for other firms.
  6. Two checkers turned the agent away. HubSpot’s form reloaded empty, and Rank Prompt showed a human-verification checkbox.
  7. The research agent saw a different market. Three Claude Opus 5.5 research agents named Flanagan Injury Law Group for the best-reviews question in 2 of 3 runs. Every checker scored Flanagan 0.

None of this means the checkers are useless. AI answers are sampled, so any single check is one draw. Our synthesis of AI recommendation research found that two answers to the same question listed the same brands fewer than once in 100 runs (SparkToro, 2,961 runs), and that at least seven runs per prompt are needed before a brand’s share settles (University of St. Gallen). A free checker gives you one run. In our Reddit study of how marketers measure AI visibility, 55 of 308 threads questioned the cost or accuracy of AI visibility tools and 40 described answers that change between runs. This test shows what that looks like in practice.

Which free AI visibility checker is best?

In this test, Geoptie was the most informative free checker, because Geoptie shows the list of firms each AI engine named. Geoptie’s score moved from day to day, so run Geoptie several times and read the lists, not the score. Semrush gave a database view in about 10 seconds, limited to 3 free checks a day. Loamly was not usable here, because no prompts ran in any of its 11 reports. HubSpot’s AI Search Grader and Rank Prompt returned nothing to an AI agent.

How to use any free checker, based on these runs:

  • Run it at least three times, on different days. One Geoptie run would have told Anesi Ozmon it was invisible; the next day’s run said Claude ranked it #2.
  • Read who is named instead of you. The lists show which competitors and directories the engines lean on. The score hides that.
  • Check what question was asked. Geoptie does not show its prompt, and its competitor lists for Anesi Ozmon leaned to workers’ compensation firms and for McCreadyLaw to Indiana firms. A checker that asks the wrong question measures the wrong market.
  • Ask a review-focused question too. The one firm of ours that the research agent recommended came up only for “best reviews.”
  • Compare a live checker with a database checker. Semrush and Geoptie measure different things, so agreement between them means more than either alone.
  • Make sure AI crawlers can read your site. Can AI read my site? is our free open-source checker for that.

How much do repeat runs of the same checker disagree?

Enough to change the verdict for some firms. Geoptie, run on the same 10 firms on October 22 and October 23, 2026, changed its score for 4 of them: Malman Law went from 33 to 60, Anesi Ozmon from 0 to 33, McCreadyLaw from 28 to 27, and Saks, Robinson & Rittenberg from 55 to 53. Across the three engines that returned answers, 4 of 30 engine results flipped between named and not named (13%, with a 95% interval of 5% to 30%).

The lists behind the scores moved more than the scores did. For each firm and engine we compared the two days’ lists of firm names. Of the 389 distinct names that appeared in either day’s list, 152 (39%) appeared in both. By engine: ChatGPT 21 of 86, Perplexity 55 of 123, Claude 76 of 180. Claude’s longer lists are sometimes cut off on the page with “+N more results,” so the Claude figure counts only the names shown.

Geoptie also returned one Perplexity list twice for two different firms. On October 22 the Perplexity answer for 844SeeMike and the Perplexity answer for Disparti Law Group listed the same 11 firms in the same order (844SeeMike, Disparti). That suggests both checks sent the same question, but Geoptie never shows the prompt it sent, so we cannot confirm it.

Loamly’s repeat run did not help. The agent re-ran Loamly for Malman Law on October 23. The report regenerated with progress stages (Website Analysis, Query Generation, AI Platform Queries, Results Analysis) instead of loading a cached copy, and ended in the same place: 0%, 0 prompts tested, 0 AI answers captured. Only the site-readiness score changed, from 75 to 76 (October 22, October 23).

Semrush has one run per firm so far, so it has no repeat to compare.

How much do the checkers disagree with each other?

They roughly agree on the most visible firms and often disagree on the rest. Semrush and Geoptie both found Malman Law and McCreadyLaw visible, and both found Flanagan Injury Law Group and The Horwitz Law Group invisible. They disagreed on Disparti Law Group (Semrush score 33 with 93 mentions, Geoptie 0 on both days) and on 844SeeMike (Semrush 19 with 11 mentions, Geoptie 0 on both days). Loamly said 0% for all 10 firms. An AI agent’s own research named only Flanagan Injury Law Group, a firm every checker scored 0.

Semrush and Geoptie measure different things

Semrush looks up a domain in its database of more than 26 million prompts and responses. Most of the mentions Semrush counted came from Google’s AI Overviews: 159 of the 280 mentions across the four firms with any (57%), and AI Overviews were the largest single platform for three of those four. For all four, the top “best performing topic” names the firm or a person behind it: “Steve Malman Family and Legal Profile,” “Mike Agruss Law and Legal Services” (for 844SeeMike), “Disparti Law Group Legal Services and Reviews” and “Mccready Law and Injury Attorneys.” So part of a Semrush score reflects answers to questions that already name the firm. Semrush’s most-cited pages were often blog posts far from hiring a lawyer, such as a post on the stages of sepsis for Malman Law and one on when a child can sit in the front seat for 844SeeMike.

Geoptie asks AI engines a category question that Geoptie infers from the website, and shows who was named. Whether a firm counts as visible then depends on which question Geoptie picked. Anesi Ozmon’s runs leaned to workers’ compensation firms. McCreadyLaw’s leaned to Indiana firms, and Semrush’s topics for McCreadyLaw include Chesterton, Indiana, too.

Disparti Law Group was named 10 times, in 9 of the runs Geoptie made for other firms, and never in its own two runs. Malman Law was named 13 times elsewhere and 3 times in its own. A firm’s own check is only as good as the question the checker chose for it.

Names can also collide. For The Horwitz Law Group, the answers were full of a different firm, Horwitz, Horwitz & Associates: on October 22 all three engines named Horwitz, Horwitz & Associates, on October 23 Perplexity did, and no engine named The Horwitz Law Group on either day. Geoptie correctly scored The Horwitz Law Group 0, but a quick reader could mistake one firm for the other. Horwitz, Horwitz & Associates was the most-named firm across all Geoptie answers: 34 times, in 18 of 20 runs. In the research agent’s 15 answers it appeared twice.

Semrush also disagreed with itself once. For The Horwitz Law Group’s domain, Semrush’s headline said 1 citation, while its own Top Cited Pages table listed pages with 2, 1 and 1 citations (raw run). The page also shows trend and score panels inside upsell blocks with the same figures for every firm; we did not count them.

Why did some checkers not work for an AI agent?

Two of the five checkers the agent tried gave the agent no result. HubSpot’s AI Search Grader reloaded with an empty form both times the agent submitted it; the page loads a bot-detection script from CHEQ, but the cause is unverified. Rank Prompt showed a Cloudflare “Verify you are human” checkbox, and the agent does not tick human-verification boxes. Neither was retried or worked around. For anyone who plans to have an AI agent run these checks, that is a finding in itself.

  • HubSpot AI Search Grader, October 22, 2026, 08:23 UTC, for Malman Law (company name “Malman Law”, location “Chicago, Illinois”, products or services “Personal injury law”, industry “Legal services”). The agent submitted the form once by clicking and once by pressing Enter after declining cookies. Both times the page reloaded with the inputs in the address and an empty form. HubSpot’s terms allow automated access at a human rate: they bar systems that send more requests “than a human can reasonably produce in the same period by using a conventional on-line web browser” (§2(v), terms last updated April 14, 2026, checked October 21, 2026). Run notes, screenshot.
  • Rank Prompt, October 22, 2026, 08:25 UTC, for Malman Law. A Cloudflare Turnstile checkbox appeared under the form and the results stayed on “Asking the engines.” Rank Prompt’s terms list as a prohibited use: “Use automated tools to access the Service in a way that exceeds reasonable use” (§8, terms last updated October 20, 2026, checked October 21, 2026). Screenshot.

Our crawler study found the same pattern on the firms’ own websites: 13 of 78 sites refused a request carrying the name GPTBot, ClaudeBot or PerplexityBot in all five runs.

Which checkers did we leave out and why?

Six. Ahrefs and Mangools ask users in their terms not to use their sites by automated means except through their APIs, so the agent did not run them; a person may run both by hand later, and this page will be updated if so. The terms of Arobis and GeoBuddy also prohibit automated use. Honeyb shows a fixed snapshot captured on October 2, 2026, so repeat runs cannot differ. Howseen’s terms say its free report was discontinued on October 10, 2026.

What did an AI agent’s own research find?

Three Claude Opus 5.5 agents researched five buyer questions on the live web on October 23, 2026, without being told which firms we tested, and named 33 different firms in 93 mentions. Only one of our 10 firms appeared: Flanagan Injury Law Group, named in 2 of 3 runs for “Which Chicago personal injury law firms have the best reviews?” with a Google rating of 4.9 from about 133 reviews, taken from Expertise.com. This is one AI agent’s research, not what ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini or the Claude app would tell a consumer.

Each agent ran at high effort in a fresh context with only web search and web fetch, and was told to answer as a careful assistant helping a consumer in Chicago, naming 3 to 10 firms per question. The three were launched together at about 19:47 UTC. The instructions, word for word, are in prompt.md, and the answers, searches and sources in run1.json, run2.json and run3.json.

The research leaders also differ from Geoptie’s. Horwitz, Horwitz & Associates and Briskman Briskman & Greenberg each appeared in 18 of 20 Geoptie runs. In the agent’s 15 answers they appeared twice and once.

How was the test run?

An AI agent (Claude, in Claude’s built-in browser, inside a Claude Code session) ran every check one at a time at human pace on October 22, 2026 (08:23 to 08:54 UTC) and October 23, 2026 (12:35 to 12:51 UTC). The agent used no AI APIs and no paid keys, declined optional cookies, entered each firm’s website or name exactly as listed below, and saved the page text of every result and a screenshot of each failure. The 10 firms are every 10th row of the 100 firms in our crawler study.

  1. Order and pace. On October 22 the agent tried HubSpot, Rank Prompt, Semrush, Geoptie and Loamly for Malman Law, then ran Geoptie for the other nine firms, Semrush for two more and Loamly for the rest, one check at a time. On October 23 the agent ran Semrush for three more firms, Geoptie for all 10 again and Loamly once more for Malman Law. Geoptie checks started about 70 seconds apart. Start times are in runs/agent/log.csv.
  2. Saving. For each run the agent saved the result text word for word in a .txt file, and a screenshot for every Geoptie and Semrush run and for the failures. Loamly reports were re-read about 2 to 14 minutes after submission to catch the final state.
  3. Terms followed. Geoptie’s terms ban only “automated access that circumvents plan allowances” (§5, terms last updated August 23, 2026). Semrush’s terms set a human-rate standard for automated systems (restriction (k), terms last updated August 25, 2026). Loamly’s terms (December 2024) have no clause on automated use. These three were checked October 21, 2026. HubSpot and Rank Prompt are quoted above.
  4. Counting. method/analyze.py parses the saved runs into data.csv (one row per run) and mentions.csv (one row per firm named in a Geoptie answer or a research answer: 541 Geoptie rows and 93 research rows). A firm is matched to our list by name fragments (for example “malman”, “disparti”, “844seemike” or “agruss”). Names in Geoptie lists cut off with “+N more results” are not counted.
  5. Comparing names. For the list overlap and the agent-research agreement, names were lowercased and stripped of a leading “The,” punctuation and LLP, LLC, P.C., PC or Ltd. Variant names such as “Katz Friedman” and the full “Katz, Friedman, Eisenstein, Johnson, Bareck & Bertuca” still count as different, so overlap is slightly undercounted.
  6. The interval. The 95% interval for 4 flips in 30 engine results is a Wilson score interval. The 30 are three engines (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude) times 10 firms; Google is left out because it never returned a list.

p = k/n = 4/30 = 0.133; z = 1.96
interval = [p + z²/2n ± z·√(p(1−p)/n + z²/4n²)] ÷ (1 + z²/n)
= 0.053 to 0.297, about 5% to 30%

The interval treats the 30 engine results as independent draws, which they are not quite: they come from 10 firms and one checker. Read it as a rough guide to how unsure 30 observations leave us.

What about open-source AI visibility trackers?

We read the setup instructions of three open-source trackers, Elmo, OneGlanse and Gego, on October 21, 2026, but did not install them. All three need paid AI API keys or accounts with the AI products before they return a first result, and the agent does not enter real API keys or sign in to accounts. Elmo and OneGlanse are MIT-licensed; Gego is GPL-3.0.
