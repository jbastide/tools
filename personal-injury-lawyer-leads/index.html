Personal injury lead cost: bought leads, Local Services Ads and Google Ads

A bought personal injury lead costs $100 to $150 for general injury and $350 to $550 for a car accident at PinPoint, and $125 to $865 at Injury Lead Gen, by those lead vendors’ own published prices (read October 30, 2026; PinPoint’s page is undated, Injury Lead Gen’s was updated June 2, 2026). For Local Services Ads, two injury firm owners in this site’s data reported paying about $220 to $300 a lead; for Google Search ads, the vendor benchmark median is $159.17 a lead for personal injury (LocaliQ, campaigns from April 2022 to March 2023). None of those prices tells you what a signed case costs: one injury firm in this site’s data reported Local Services Ads costs of $843 to $5,719 per signed case between December 2024 and April 2025.

Personal injury lawyer leads are inquiries from people who may have an injury claim. A firm can buy them from a lead vendor, where they are also sold as personal injury attorney leads, or generate its own with ads, search and referrals. This guide sets the vendors’ published prices and the ad platforms’ benchmarks beside what injury firm owners reported paying and signing, and compares the sources by the one figure that makes them comparable: cost per signed case.

By Copy for Originals · Published October 13, 2026 · Last updated October 31, 2026

How much do personal injury leads cost?

A bought personal injury lead costs $100 to $865 at PinPoint and Injury Lead Gen, two lead vendors that publish their own prices (PinPoint’s page undated, Injury Lead Gen’s updated June 2, 2026, both read October 30, 2026), with car accident leads at $350 to $550 and $360 to $475. Injury firm owners reported paying about $220 to $300 for a Local Services Ads lead, and the vendor benchmark medians for a Google Search ads lead are $159.17 for personal injury (LocaliQ, campaigns from 2022 to 2023) and $131.63 for all legal services (WordStream, campaigns from 2025 to 2026).

The kinds of figure are not the same thing. A vendor’s own price is what that vendor says it charges, on its own sales page. A vendor estimate is a marketing company’s range for the market, not a rate card: Retainer Reach and Rankings.io give market ranges, and Best Case Leads, which sells only exclusive leads, says what personal injury leads “typically cost” without saying whether that is its own price. A benchmark median comes from an ad platform’s customer campaigns. LocaliQ’s legal figures rest on 256 US search campaigns that ran from April 1, 2022 to March 31, 2023, with at least 10 in each subcategory, so the personal injury figures may rest on as few as 10. WordStream’s rest on 13,474 campaigns from April 1, 2025 to March 31, 2026, with at least 52 in each subcategory, and its previous edition (campaigns April 2024 to March 2025) gave the identical $131.63, which the newer edition does not explain. Owner-reported figures are rows in this site’s data file, one owner each.

The two click prices measure different things too. When injury owners on r/LawFirm cite clicks of $200 or more, that is what an owner paid for their own keywords in their own market. LocaliQ’s $9.30 is a median across its personal injury campaigns from 2022 to 2023, and WordStream’s $9.87 a median across all legal services. Plan with the cost per click in your own account.

What a Local Services Ads lead charges for. Google says you pay for valid leads, that lead prices may vary with your location, the job type, the type of lead or your bidding mode, and that message leads are typically priced lower than phone leads, although not always. A phone lead is valid when you answer or call back and speak with the customer, or when you miss a call during business hours and the caller stays on the line for more than 20 seconds. Leads judged invalid or low quality at first contact are not charged; charged leads are reassessed and may be credited automatically, in most cases within 30 days, and the original charge still appears on the invoice. Google may occasionally credit a lead you report as poor quality in that lead’s feedback survey, and it no longer credits “job type not serviced” or “geo not serviced” leads. Follow-up calls or messages from the same number or email within 15 days are not charged again.

Change under way, checked October 30, 2026. Google’s help page says that over the upcoming months existing Local Services Ads campaigns will be automatically migrated to a Performance Max campaign type optimized for pay-per-lead goals. Its timeline starts in August 2026 with select home and storefront service advertisers in the United States, such as plumbing, HVAC, roofing and moving; expands in late 2026 to broader advertiser groups, including service-area businesses without physical storefronts; and moves non-U.S. accounts and all remaining business categories not migrated in 2026 in 2027. That page does not name law firms or legal services. Two other Google help pages mention “professional services”: the supported-verticals page lists them in phase 2, late 2026, and the overview of pay-per-lead goals says professional services will become available for pay-per-lead goals in Performance Max in 2027. So Google’s pages put professional services in late 2026 on one page and 2027 on another, and none of them names law firms. Google says you still pay only for valid leads, the ads still show only on Google Search and Maps, and manual bidding, such as setting a maximum cost per lead, is no longer supported in Google Ads. For these campaigns Google says its systems filter out spam and low-quality interactions, such as solicitation, wrong numbers and duplicate callers within 15 days, so you are not charged for them. Two injury owners described prices they were quoted and turned down. One was offered “qualified” car accident calls at $1,500 each and worked that out to about $3,000 per signed case using the vendor’s claimed signing rate of 40% to 60%. Another was offered the contact details of a provider’s interested patients at $100 to $200 each and dropped the idea over the solicitation and ethics risk.

A third injury owner reported spending $120,000 a year on lead generation companies and some social media, with about half of the firm’s cases coming from marketing and results that went up and down.

The owner figures are rows in the open data file of How much do legal marketing agencies charge? That study’s cross-practice prices are not personal injury prices: its bought-lead range of $40 to $150 comes from four owners in bankruptcy, criminal defense and family law, and its Google Search ads range of $80 to $1,000 from four reports, none from an injury firm.

What does a signed personal injury case cost?

In this site’s data, one small injury firm reported what its Local Services Ads cost per signed case: $1,449 in December 2024, $843 in February 2025 and $5,719 in April 2025. Another firm, mostly malpractice and birth injury, spent $75,000 on all marketing for 22 cases, $3,409 a case. Those are owner reports, not a market rate, and the cost per signed case figures lead vendors publish are worked examples with hypothetical numbers.

How do you compare personal injury lead sources?

Compare personal injury lead sources by cost per signed case: cost per lead ÷ the share of leads that are real potential clients ÷ the share of those that sign. A $100 lead where one in five is real and one in four of those signs costs $2,000 per signed case.

Judge a source on what you can count yourself: the price per lead, the share of leads your intake could reach, the share of those that were real potential clients, and the share that signed. The price alone misleads: the firm above paid about the same per Local Services Ads lead in February and April 2025, and its cost per signed case went from $843 to $5,719.

Treat a vendor’s signing rate as a claim. Best Case Leads says exclusive leads cost 3 to 5 times more than shared leads and convert at 15% to 30%, without a source or sample; Injury Lead Gen lists 15% to 25% for Local Services Ads leads, also without a source; and a vendor told one injury owner that 40% to 60% of its qualified calls sign. Ask for signed cases per hundred leads, then count your own.

To run the formula on your own numbers, the Local Services Ads vs Google Ads guide has a calculator that compares two sources and gives the share of junk leads at which they break even.

Should a personal injury firm buy leads or generate its own?

Compare the two by cost per signed case, not by the price of a lead. A bought lead’s price says nothing about whether it signs, and in 15 of the 20 r/LawFirm threads where a law firm owner described a lead vendor, the owner reported junk leads. Leads a firm generates itself come with the ad account, the data and the phone number, which the firm keeps.

How many bought personal injury leads turn into signed cases?

Few, in the reports we have. One personal injury owner bought 20 leads and signed one case, another bought 8 and could not reach any of them, and a third paying $10,000 a month said the leads were worth the money but brought smaller cases. Can a personal injury lawyer pay for leads?

Under the ABA Model Rules a lawyer may pay for leads, with conditions. Comment 5 to Model Rule 7.2 says a lawyer may pay others for generating client leads as long as the lead generator does not recommend the lawyer, the payment is consistent with the rules on dividing fees and professional independence, and the lead generator’s communications are not misleading. Each state adopts its own version, so check yours.

Model Rule 7.2(b) starts from a prohibition: “A lawyer shall not compensate, give or promise anything of value to a person for recommending the lawyer’s services,” with listed exceptions that include paying “the reasonable costs of advertisements or communications permitted by this Rule.”

Comment 5 then draws the line for lead vendors: “To comply with Rule 7.1, a lawyer must not pay a lead generator that states, implies, or creates a reasonable impression that it is recommending the lawyer, is making the referral without payment from the lawyer, or has analyzed a person’s legal problems when determining which lawyer should receive the referral.”

In our thread data, 4 of the 20 threads about a lead vendor mentioned a vendor tactic that might break a rule. One injury owner turned down a proposal to receive the contact details of a provider’s interested patients at $100 to $200 each, over the risk that it was solicitation.

These are the ABA’s model rules, not your state’s. This page does not cover any state’s version or its rules on solicitation and referral fees.

What should a personal injury firm ask a lead vendor?

Ask a lead vendor six things before paying: whether each lead is sold to one firm or several, how the lead was generated, what counts as a bad lead and how credits work, how long the contract runs, whether you see the person’s original inquiry, and how many signed cases its other personal injury clients get per hundred leads.

  1. One firm or several? In 3 of the 20 lead vendor threads, owners said their leads had been sold to other firms too.
  2. How was the lead generated? Ask to see the ad or page the person responded to. Under Comment 5 it must not suggest the vendor is recommending you or has assessed the case.
  3. What is a bad lead, and what happens then? Get the credit or refund rule in writing. One owner said most of 20 leads were junk or were refunded. PinPoint, for one, says it doesn’t refund leads that simply don’t sign.
  4. How long is the contract? In 2 of the 20 threads the owner described being locked into a long one.
  5. Do you see the original inquiry? A name and a phone number tell intake less than what the person actually wrote or said.
  6. What do other injury firms sign? One vendor claimed 40% to 60% of its qualified calls sign. Ask for signed cases per hundred leads, then count your own.

The questions are ours. They follow from what owners reported, not from a rule.

How does a personal injury firm generate its own leads?

A personal injury firm generates its own leads from Google Search ads, Local Services Ads, organic search and its Google Business Profile, and referrals. In Chicago, 48 of 100 visible injury firms ran Google ads, and advertisers had a median of 219 Google reviews against 96 for firms that didn’t advertise.

  • Google Search ads. The firm pays per click and owns the account. The personal injury Google Ads guide covers costs, keywords and who else is bidding, and the budget guide gives the minimum monthly spend.
  • Local Services Ads. The firm pays per lead that Google counts as valid. The Local Services Ads guide covers which leads Google credits.
  • Organic search and the Google Business Profile. In Chicago the map results with review counts sat above every other result, and 74 of 100 injury firms listed themselves as open 24 hours.
  • Referrals. One injury owner spending about $50,000 a month on marketing said referrals were still 75% of the business.

The Chicago figures are from the Chicago personal injury lead generation study of 100 firms, which also lists eight actions that need no new ad spend.

Why do personal injury leads go cold?

Personal injury leads go cold when nobody answers or calls back quickly, whichever source they came from. In CallRail’s December 2024 survey, 96% of the 49 respondents, law firm and marketing agency staff, said a slow response or no response can lead to lost cases, and CallRail’s own call data showed 28% of legal industry calls were missed.

The respondents worked full-time at a law firm (79%) or at a marketing agency that works with law firms (21%), and 49 is a small sample, so treat the 96% as a strong majority, not a precise figure. CallRail’s report gives no period or size for its call data. Other evidence points the same way:

  • In the secret-shopper test in Clio’s 2024 Legal Trends Report, 48% of law firms neither answered the phone nor called back, as summarized by the Illinois Supreme Court Commission on Professionalism.
  • In 207 of the lowest-rated Google reviews at 11 Chicago injury firms, 38% describe no callback or an unreachable lawyer.
  • The injury owner who bought 8 marketplace leads could not reach one of them by call, text or email.

Shared leads make speed matter more. Anthony Higman, CEO at Adsquire, is quoted in CallRail’s 2026 report, which is based on a survey of 100 legal professionals in October 2025, saying Google’s “Local Services Ads have made it easier than ever for one lead to message the same issue to four different law firms at once. Everyone gets charged for the lead, so the game now is simple: the fastest follow-up wins.” That is one person’s view, not survey data.

A firm that cannot answer within minutes will lose bought leads and its own leads alike, so fix the phone before buying more of either.

When do bought leads make sense for a personal injury firm?

Bought leads make sense as a test with a spending cap, a short contract and a count of signed cases at the end. The owners who were satisfied with a lead vendor described a follow-up system or could say what the leads had produced.

The satisfied owners in the data had three things in common. They knew the price per lead, they had someone calling every lead back, and they could say what the leads produced: one family law solo traced $693.98 of marketplace leads to four clients and $8,000 in fees, then stopped when later spending returned less.

The dissatisfied ones mostly could not reach the leads, or reached people who were not potential clients. So a test should measure both: how many leads could be reached, and how many of those signed.

Know the size of the first commitment before you start. PinPoint, for example, says on its undated sales page (read October 30, 2026) that its typical starting order is roughly $8,000 to $10,000, prepaid and drawn down as validated leads are delivered.

Limitations

  • The owner reports come from public r/LawFirm threads from October 2023 to October 2026. They are what individual owners wrote, not a sample of personal injury firms, and people with a bad experience are more likely to post.
  • Most personal injury figures here rest on one owner each. The table of bought-lead outcomes has five reports.
  • Vendor names are withheld in the owner reports, as in the studies the figures come from. The vendors named in the cost table are named for their own published prices and estimates. We did not test any vendor.
  • Vendor prices and estimates are as their pages read on October 30, 2026, and PinPoint’s page is undated. Benchmark medians come from ad platforms’ customer campaigns, and LocaliQ’s personal injury figures are from campaigns in 2022 and 2023.
  • The 20 lead vendor threads cover all practice areas, not only personal injury.
  • The ABA pages carry no revision date. No state’s rules are covered.
  • The Chicago figures are for one city.
  • Not legal advice.
