Does Google record calls to law firms from its ads?

Google records calls to law firms that come through a Google forwarding number, in the United States and Canada. That covers Local Services Ads and, since Google changed a default setting, call ads and call assets in Google Ads. This guide quotes what Google says it records, how long it keeps recordings in legal categories, what callers hear, what a firm can switch off, and what two state bars have said about it.

By Copy for Originals · Published October 13, 2026 · Last updated October 13, 2026

Educational only, not legal advice. This page quotes Google’s help pages and two state bar publications as read on October 13, 2026. Whether a recorded intake call affects confidentiality or privilege in your state is a question for your own counsel and your state bar.

Does Google record calls to law firms from its ads?

Yes, in the United States and Canada, when the call goes through a Google forwarding number. Google’s Local Services help page says all inbound calls through the forwarding number are recorded, and its Google Ads help page says the call recording setting is now enabled by default. A firm can turn recording off in Google Ads. The Local Services help pages we read describe no way to turn it off.

Does Google record Local Services Ads calls to lawyers?

Yes. Google’s Local Services help page says that in the US and Canada all inbound calls from customers through the Google forwarding number are recorded by Google, and that customers are told by a pre-recorded message. Google keeps recordings and messages in legal categories for 60 days.

The sentence on Google’s “Manage leads and jobs” page reads: “in the US and Canada, all inbound calls from customers via the Google forwarding number are recorded by Google to improve quality and support policies and research (and customers are notified of this by a pre-recorded message immediately before you hear the ‘Call from Google’ notification).”

The number on a Local Services ad is always a Google forwarding number, so this covers every call from the ad. The same page says call recordings and messages are not available for health care categories or tax specialists. It names no exception for lawyers.

Google’s “About Local Services data” page lists what Google does with them. It says Google “reviews emails, texts, or call recordings routed through Google’s system on a limited basis” for these purposes: resolving billing disputes, billing advertisers for leads, enforcing policies, detecting spam and unauthorized behavior, collecting customer feedback, product improvement, and monitoring customer and provider experience.

Change under way, checked October 13, 2026. Google is phasing out the separate Local Services Ads platform and moving existing campaigns into Google Ads as “Performance Max campaigns with pay-per-lead goals,” with professional services listed for late 2026 on one page and 2027 on another. Recording continues after the move. Google says past lead history, including “older call recordings,” transfers to the Leads page in Google Ads, and its page on leads in the new campaign type says call recordings are unavailable only for tax specialists, US health care categories and Europe. Google’s pages do not say whether the Google Ads call recording setting described below will govern these campaigns, so check that setting again after your account moves. The Local Services Ads vs Google Ads guide for lawyers covers the move.

Does Google Ads record calls from call ads and call assets?

Yes, by default, when call reporting is on. Google’s help page says the call recording setting is now enabled by default so that its AI can qualify call leads, and that it stays off for advertisers who turned it off earlier and for businesses identified as healthcare or financial services. Legal services are not named as an exception.

Google’s page on call recording settings reads: “The call recording setting is now enabled by default to allow for AI-powered lead qualification.” Recording applies only when all of these are true:

  • Both phone numbers are in the United States or Canada.
  • The call goes through a Google forwarding number, which requires call reporting to be turned on.
  • The call is to a call ad or a call asset, or comes from a website visit. Calls from location assets are not recorded.

Google’s AI then listens to the recording. Google’s page on AI-qualified call leads says that when call recording is on, “all calls will be recorded and evaluated by AI,” and that call reports show “a concise AI-generated summary of the call and specific hashtags (for example, ‘#HighIntent,’ ‘#ConsultationScheduled’).” For a law firm, that means a written summary of what a caller said about their matter appears inside the Google Ads account.

Google says recordings “are used to evaluate lead quality, monitor spam and fraud, and improve the accuracy of your conversion reporting.”

What do callers hear before a recorded call?

Callers hear an automated notice before a recorded call connects. For Local Services Ads, Google says customers are notified by a pre-recorded message. For Google Ads, Google says callers hear a short automated message at the beginning of the call saying the conversation will be recorded for quality purposes.

Neither help page gives the exact words of the notice. A January 2021 article by the State Bar of Wisconsin’s ethics counsel reported the Local Services messages as they were described to Wisconsin lawyers at the time. The caller heard: “Google and its partners may record or return all calls for quality and research purposes, subject to our privacy policies. This call is not confidential.” The law firm heard: “Call from Google. This call is recorded and may not be attorney-client privileged.” That wording is from 2021 and came through marketing consultants, so call your own ad to hear what plays now.

Google’s page adds that a firm may not hear the “Call from Google” message at all if it has call forwarding or VoIP set up on its business number. Staff may then not know which calls are recorded.

The notice to the caller is Google’s. The notice to the firm’s own people is the firm’s job: Google’s Call Ads Supplemental Terms say the advertiser has told its “agents, employees, and any other party that may participate” that calls may be monitored or recorded by Google, and has their express consent.

How long does Google keep recordings of calls to law firms?

Google says Local Services recordings and messages in legal verticals are retained for 60 days, against up to 180 days for other categories. In Google Ads, recorded calls can be played for 30 days after the call. Google’s help pages do not say how long Google itself keeps a Google Ads recording after that.

The sentences on Google’s “About Local Services data” page read: “Call recordings and messages are retained up to 180 days before they are destroyed. Recordings and messages in legal verticals are retained for 60 days.”

For Google Ads, the “Play your call recordings” page reads: “Recorded calls are available for 30 days after the date of the call.” It also says recordings can only be played inside Google Ads and “are not available for download.”

Anything the firm records itself, with its own phone system or a call tracking service, is kept under the firm’s own settings. Google’s data page says providers “may choose to independently record calls” and that Google “is not responsible for any data stored outside of Google.”

Who can listen to the recordings?

The firm can listen, anyone with the right access to the firm’s Google account can listen, and Google reviews recordings for stated purposes. In Google Ads, users with Admin or Standard access and manager-account users with Admin access can play recordings, which includes an agency that manages the account.

Can a law firm turn off Google’s call recording?

In Google Ads, yes: an Admin can set Call recording to Off under Admin, Account settings, Call ads. A firm can also turn off call reporting so its own number shows on ads, and then Google does not track or record the call. For Local Services Ads, the help pages describe no setting to turn recording off.

  1. Turn off recording, keep call tracking. In Google Ads go to Admin, Account settings, open “Call ads,” and set “Call recording” to Off. Google’s page says the system then falls back “to using call duration to measure conversions,” and its AI stops analyzing conversations.
  2. Turn off call reporting. Google’s call reporting page says turning it off “shows your direct business number but prevents Google Ads from tracking calls.” The firm then needs its own way to count calls from ads.
  3. Check that it stayed off. Google says recording remains off for advertisers who turned it off before the default changed. Look at the setting anyway, on every account the firm advertises from.

Turning recording off has a cost. With it on, Google counts only calls its AI judges to be qualified leads; with it off, any call over the set length counts. Our campaign checklist uses 60 seconds as that length.

For Local Services Ads the forwarding number is how Google counts and bills leads, and the two help pages we read offer no recording setting. A firm that will not accept recorded calls can decide not to run Local Services Ads; that is the choice North Carolina’s committee was weighing.

Does Google’s call recording break a lawyer’s duty of confidentiality?

No state ethics opinion we found says it does. North Carolina’s ethics committee proposed an opinion in 2021 that would have barred lawyers from a pay-per-lead advertising program because the platform recorded and kept calls, and withdrew it in April 2022. Wisconsin’s State Bar ethics counsel wrote in January 2021 that the answer depends on whether the caller is a prospective client.

North Carolina

The North Carolina State Bar’s journal explained the episode in summer 2022. Proposed 2021 Formal Ethics Opinion 5 concluded that the program’s structure made a lawyer’s participation “prejudicial to the administration of justice in violation of Rule 8.4(d)” because it “does not comply with a person’s reasonable and historic expectations of privacy and exclusivity in communicating with a lawyer.”

After comments from the bar, the committee concluded “that the recordings at issue are not uniquely vulnerable to harmful third-party disclosures” and withdrew the proposal at its April 2022 meeting. The article still reminds lawyers “of their duty to make reasonable investigations into any advertising program—or technology service—before deciding whether to participate.”

Wisconsin

The State Bar of Wisconsin’s ethics counsel wrote in January 2021 that to take part lawyers had to authorize Google “to access, monitor, and record telephone calls, text messages, live chat, and other communications,” and that whether this fits the duty of confidentiality “depends whether the person contacting the lawyer through the LSA is a prospective client.” The article also notes that the first call comes through a number Google controls, so the lawyer cannot add their own warning before it connects. It is commentary from the bar’s ethics counsel, not a formal opinion.

The rule both rely on

ABA Model Rule 1.18(b) says: “Even when no client-lawyer relationship ensues, a lawyer who has learned information from a prospective client shall not use or reveal that information,” with limited exceptions. The North Carolina article quotes the comment to that rule: to avoid creating a duty, “a lawyer has an affirmative obligation to warn the person that a communication with the lawyer will not create a client-lawyer relationship and information conveyed to the lawyer will not be confidential or privileged.”

Neither publication says a recorded call loses attorney-client privilege, and neither says it keeps it. Privilege is a question of evidence law in your state, and this page does not answer it.

What should a law firm do about recorded calls?

Find out which of the firm’s numbers are Google forwarding numbers, tell the staff who answer them, keep the first call to scheduling and conflict information, decide whether to leave Google Ads recording on, and check who has access to the account. Then confirm the plan with the lawyer who handles the firm’s ethics questions.

  1. List the numbers. Write down every number a caller can reach the firm on from an ad: the Local Services number, Google Ads call ads and call assets, and the number on the landing page. Mark which are Google forwarding numbers.
  2. Tell the people who answer. Google’s terms assume staff have been told and have consented. If the firm uses call forwarding or VoIP, staff may not hear “Call from Google,” so tell them which lines are recorded.
  3. Keep the first call short. The North Carolina article says that with trained staff, “appointment-setting phone calls should neither create a reasonable expectation of privacy nor delve into information beyond what is necessary to avoid conflicts of interest.” Take the name, the contact details and what is needed for a conflict check, then move the substance to a line the firm controls.
  4. Give the warning the rule describes. Ask your counsel what intake should say about confidentiality and about no client-lawyer relationship being formed on the first call.
  5. Decide on the Google Ads setting. Leave recording on for better lead counting, or turn it off, knowing what each costs.
  6. Check who has access. Anyone with Admin or Standard access to the Google Ads account can play recordings and read the AI summaries.
  7. Check your own recording. If the firm records calls itself or through a call tracking service, state consent laws for recording apply to that, and they differ by state.

The list follows from Google’s pages and the North Carolina article. It is not legal advice, and item 4 in particular is for your own counsel.

Limitations

  • Google changes its help pages and defaults. Every quotation is from the page as it read on October 13, 2026; check the setting in your own account.
  • Google’s pages do not give the date the Google Ads default changed, the exact words of the caller notice, or how long Google keeps a Google Ads recording after the 30-day playback period.
  • We read two Local Services help pages. A recording setting could exist elsewhere in the product; neither page describes one.
  • The Call Ads Supplemental Terms page says it currently applies to advertisers in the United States.
  • We found two state bar publications on the question, neither a formal opinion in force. Other states may have guidance we did not find.
  • State laws on recording calls and the law of privilege are not covered.
  • Not legal advice.
