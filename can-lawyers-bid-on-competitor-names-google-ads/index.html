Can a law firm bid on a competitor’s name in Google Ads? The rules by state

Bidding on a competitor’s name means adding another law firm’s name as a Google Ads keyword, so your ad shows when someone searches for that firm. Lawyers also call it competitive keyword advertising or conquesting. This guide lists the seven states with a published position, quotes each one, and covers the court cases, Google’s own policy, and what a firm can do when the name being bid on is its own.

By Copy for Originals · Published October 13, 2026 · Last updated October 13, 2026

Educational only, not legal advice. This page quotes public ethics opinions, court rulings and Google’s policies as read on October 13, 2026. Copy for Originals is a Google Ads consultant, not a law firm. Your state bar’s rules and your own counsel decide what your firm may do.

Can a law firm bid on a competitor’s name in Google Ads?

It depends on the state. Ethics opinions in North Carolina, Ohio and Michigan say a lawyer may not use another lawyer’s name as a keyword. Texas, South Carolina and New Jersey allow it with conditions, and Florida’s rule turns on whether the ad makes clear whose ad it is. Google itself does not restrict using a trademark as a keyword.

Which states prohibit bidding on another lawyer’s name?

North Carolina, Ohio and Michigan have ethics opinions saying a lawyer may not use another lawyer’s or law firm’s name as a keyword. North Carolina and Ohio treat it as dishonest conduct under Rule 8.4(c), and Michigan requires the other lawyer’s express consent.

North Carolina

North Carolina State Bar 2010 Formal Ethics Opinion 14, adopted April 27, 2012, concludes: “it is a violation of Rule 8.4(c) for a lawyer to select another lawyer’s name to be used in his own keyword advertising.” The opinion reasons that buying the recognition attached to another lawyer’s name to send consumers to a competing website “is neither fair nor straightforward.”

North Carolina extended the rule to firm trade names in 2023 Formal Ethics Opinion 4, adopted January 19, 2024. Its summary reads: “the intentional selection of another lawyer’s unique firm trade name in a keyword advertisement campaign is prohibited, but that prohibition does not apply when the trade name is also a common search term.” In the opinion’s example, a lawyer may not select the unique trade name “Strike Three Divorce Lawyers,” but may select “Durham family lawyers” even though another firm uses that phrase as its trade name.

Ohio

Ohio Board of Professional Conduct Opinion 2021-04, issued June 11, 2021, states in its syllabus: “A lawyer or law firm may not purchase the name of another lawyer or law firm for use in competitive keyword online advertising.” The opinion finds the purchase itself is not a false communication under Rule 7.1, but calls it “an act that is designed to deceive an Internet user and thus contrary to Prof.Cond.R. 8.4(c).” It adds that a lawyer who tries and fails to divert the searcher has still attempted a violation under Rule 8.4(a). The opinion describes itself as a nonbinding advisory opinion.

Michigan

State Bar of Michigan Opinion RI-385, dated November 18, 2022, states in its syllabus: “An attorney may not utilize a keyword advertising campaign that involves using the name of another attorney, law firm or attorney’s or law firm’s tradenames without the express consent of the other attorney or law firm.” The opinion cites Michigan Rules of Professional Conduct 7.1(a), 7.5(d) and 8.4(b), and covers trade names as well as personal names.

Which states allow bidding on another lawyer’s name?

Texas, South Carolina and New Jersey allow a lawyer to buy a competitor’s name as a keyword. Texas says the ad must not be false or misleading or suggest the lawyers are affiliated, South Carolina bars derogatory messages and disguised redirect links, and New Jersey requires a disclaimer on the landing page.

Texas

Texas Professional Ethics Committee Opinion 661, issued July 2016, concludes: “A lawyer does not violate the Texas Disciplinary Rules of Professional Conduct by simply using the name of a competing lawyer or law firm as a keyword in the implementation of an advertising service offered by a major search-engine company.” The ad itself still has to follow the advertising rules: it must not be false or misleading, and the lawyer must not hold out as a partner, shareholder or associate of the other lawyer. The opinion also notes that keyword advertising “may be subject to other requirements or prohibitions imposed by federal or state law or by professional ethics rules of other jurisdictions.”

South Carolina

South Carolina Bar Ethics Advisory Opinion 20-01 concludes that “a lawyer may purchase an internet competitive advertising keyword that is the name of another lawyer or law firm, in order to display a ‘sponsored’ website advertisement.” The lawyer “should use care to ensure that no derogatory or uncivil message is conveyed,” and “surreptitious redirection from a competitor’s website to a lawyer’s own web page via a hyperlink is prohibited.” The committee notes that it has no disciplinary authority.

New Jersey

The New Jersey Supreme Court decided In re Opinion No. 735 on May 22, 2025, and held “that the practice of purchasing a competitor’s name as a keyword does not, in itself, constitute a violation of the RPCs.” The Court affirmed its ethics committee’s 2019 opinion and added a requirement. A lawyer who buys a competitor’s name as a keyword must put this disclaimer on any landing page the paid ad leads to:

You arrived at this page via a paid advertisement on [insert name of search engine provider] through paid keyword search results. This website and the legal business it describes are affiliated only with [insert name of purchasing attorney] and the other attorneys referenced within this website.

The committee’s separate finding that a hyperlink placed on a competitor’s name to redirect the user violates Rule 8.4(c) was not challenged and still stands. Justice Fasciale dissented and would have held that the practice violates Rule 8.4(c).

What does Florida’s rule say about ads on another lawyer’s name?

Florida does not ban buying another lawyer’s name as a keyword, but since May 2, 2022 Rule 4-7.13(b)(12) has prohibited ads that mislead a person searching for one lawyer into unknowingly contacting another. The rule’s comment says the ad is not misleading if the first text displayed is the name of the advertising lawyer or law firm.

The Florida Supreme Court added the subdivision in case No. SC20-1467, decided March 3, 2022. It lists as a deceptive and inherently misleading advertisement: “a statement or implication that another lawyer or law firm is part of, is associated with, or affiliated with the advertising law firm when that is not the case, including contact or other information presented in a way that misleads a person searching for a particular lawyer or law firm, or for information regarding a particular lawyer or law firm, to unknowingly contact a different lawyer or law firm.”

The comment adopted with the rule gives this as impermissible: “use of another lawyer or law firm name as an Internet search term that triggers the display of an advertisement that does not clearly indicate that the advertisement is for a lawyer or law firm that is not the lawyer or law firm used as the search term.” It then says how to comply: “The triggered advertisement would not be misleading if the first text displayed is the name of the advertising lawyer or law firm.” If the firm uses a trade name that does not include a partner’s name, the name of the lawyer responsible for the ad must also be in that first text.

Is bidding on a competitor law firm’s name trademark infringement?

In the two federal appeals rulings between law firms covered here, the keyword purchase alone did not decide the case; what mattered was whether searchers were likely to be confused. The Ninth Circuit found no likely confusion in Lerner & Rowe in 2024. The Fifth Circuit let a claim proceed in Adler v. McNeil in 2021 because the complaint alleged generic ads and misleading call handling in addition to the keyword purchase.

Franklin D. Azar & Associates’ suit against the Slocumb Law Firm (filed 2022)An Alabama firm allegedly ran Google ads that made callers believe they were calling Azar’s firm.Settled for $1.55 million, as reported in December 2024.

The Ninth Circuit had numbers to work with in Lerner & Rowe. Searches for “Lerner & Rowe” showed the competitor’s ad 109,322 times between 2017 and 2021. Callers mentioned Lerner & Rowe in 236 intake calls, which the court put at 0.216% of those shown the ad and called de minimis. Users clicked the competitor’s ad 7,452 times, or 6.82% of the time it was shown. A survey commissioned by the competitor found that its ads confused between 0% and 3% of consumers.

The Fifth Circuit ruled on a complaint, not on evidence. It wrote that the pleading “included factual matter beyond the mere purchase of trademarks as keywords for search-engine advertising.” Adler had also offered a survey of 400 Texas residents that, in the court’s words, “purportedly shows” that between 34% and 44% of participants clicked the ad believing it was connected to him. The ruling sent the case back to the district court; this page does not cover what happened there afterward.

The Azar firm filed a similar suit against a Denver firm in Denver District Court, BusinessDen reported on May 22, 2025. No outcome was reported in that article.

Does Google allow bidding on a competitor’s name?

Yes. Google’s trademark policy says Google Ads will not restrict using trademarks as keywords. After a trademark owner complains, Google may restrict the trademark in the text of a competitor’s ad, but not as a keyword.

Google’s Trademarks policy lists what Google Ads will not restrict after a trademark complaint, and the first item is “Using trademarks as keywords.” It also says “The trademark must be used in the ad, not only on the ad’s landing page.”

The same policy lists what Google will restrict: “Using trademarks in an ad from a direct competitor” and “Ads that use the trademark in a confusing, deceptive, or misleading way.” So a firm with a trademark can ask Google to stop a competitor from writing its name into ad headlines or descriptions, but not from bidding on the name.

Google’s policy is not a legal or ethics ruling. An ad Google accepts can still break a state’s rules.

What makes an ad on a competitor’s name risky?

Across the opinions, rules and cases on this page, the trouble comes from four things: the state the lawyer is in, an ad that doesn’t say whose it is, the competitor’s name appearing in the ad itself, and calls answered without naming the firm. The keyword purchase alone was the problem only in the three states that prohibit it.

What should a law firm check before bidding on a competitor’s name?

Check six things before bidding on a competitor’s name: your state’s position, every other state where the ad will run, that the first line of your ad is your own firm’s name, that the competitor’s name cannot appear in your ad, that the landing page says whose site it is, and that calls are answered with your firm’s name.

  1. Your state’s position. Start with the table above, then confirm with your state bar, because an opinion can be withdrawn or replaced.
  2. Every state the campaign reaches. The Texas opinion warns that other jurisdictions’ ethics rules may apply. A firm licensed or advertising in more than one state has more than one rule to meet.
  3. The first line of the ad. Put your own firm’s name first, which is what Florida’s comment asks for.
  4. Keyword insertion. Google’s help page says keyword insertion updates an ad “with the keywords in your ad group that caused your ads to show.” In an ad group that holds a competitor’s name, that feature can put the competitor’s name in your headline, so leave it out of those ads.
  5. The landing page. It should say whose firm it is at the top. In New Jersey it must carry the Court’s disclaimer.
  6. The phone. Answer with the firm’s name, on every line the ad can reach, including click-to-call numbers and any call center.

Then take the plan to the lawyer who handles your firm’s ethics questions. Nothing on this list is a substitute for that.

Can a law firm’s ad show on a competitor’s name by accident?

Yes. A broad or phrase match keyword such as “car accident lawyer” can match a search that also contains another firm’s name, so the ad shows without the firm ever choosing that name. The search terms report shows when this has happened, and adding the competitor’s name as a negative keyword stops it.

Google’s help page on match types says a broad match keyword matches “additional related searches” beyond the ones phrase and exact match would catch. A search such as “Smith and Jones car accident lawyer” contains the keyword and a firm’s name.

None of the ethics opinions read for this page mentions broad match. The North Carolina opinions speak of a lawyer who intentionally selects the name. A firm in a state that prohibits the practice can remove the question by adding the names of local competitors as negative keywords. That is a Google Ads practice, not a rule from any opinion. The Negative Keyword Finder reads a search terms report in the browser, so you can look for other firms’ names in the searches you paid for.

What can a law firm do if a competitor is bidding on its name?

A law firm whose name is being bid on can document the ads, see which advertisers appear on its name with the auction insights report, run a campaign on its own name, and file a trademark complaint with Google if its name appears in the competitor’s ad text. Whether to go to the state bar or to court is a question for the firm’s own lawyer.

  1. Document it. Search your firm’s name in a private browser window, on a phone and a computer, and save dated screenshots of each ad, its headline and the page it leads to.
  2. See who is in the auction. If you run a campaign on your own name, Google’s auction insights report “lets you compare your performance with other advertisers who are participating in the same auctions that you are.” It lists the advertisers, not their keywords, and shows nothing when impression share is under 10%.
  3. Check the ad text. If your name is in the competitor’s headline or description and you hold a trademark, Google’s Trademarks policy has a complaint form. If your name is only the keyword, Google will not act.
  4. Run your own name. A campaign on your own firm name puts your ad on the same searches, with your name in the first line.
  5. Call the number. In Adler and in the Azar suit the complaint was about how calls were answered. Call the number in the ad and write down how the person answering identifies the firm.
  6. Ask your lawyer about the state bar and the courts. In North Carolina, Ohio and Michigan the opinions treat the keyword selection itself as improper. Elsewhere, the cases above turned on evidence of confusion.
