What’s going wrong?

What PPC Troubleshooter does

PPC Troubleshooter diagnoses five common Google Ads problems: spending money with zero conversions, a budget used up by a few expensive clicks, results that dropped after a good start, a rising cost per click, and ads that barely spend. For each problem, PPC Troubleshooter asks follow-up questions one at a time, then lists the likely causes in three groups: Fix first, Check and Good news. Each cause comes with the steps to take. Where your numbers matter, PPC Troubleshooter runs the math and shows it as a picture so you can tell bad luck from a real problem.

Why did my Google Ads campaign spend money and get zero conversions?

With only a few dozen clicks, zero conversions is often bad luck rather than a broken campaign. If 2.5 out of every 100 visitors become leads, a typical rate for lead gen, each click has a 97.5% chance of not converting. The chance that every one of 20 clicks misses is 0.97520, or about 60%. So more than half of perfectly healthy campaigns get zero leads from their first 20 clicks.

PPC Troubleshooter uses the formula chance of zero = (1 − conversion rate)clicks. It calls the result normal bad luck when that chance is 25% or more, unlucky when it is between 5% and 25%, and not luck when it is under 5%. At a 2.5% conversion rate, the tipping point is 119 clicks: past that, zero conversions happens less than 5% of the time, so something is broken. Common causes are conversion tracking that isn’t recording, irrelevant search terms, and a landing page that doesn’t ask for the lead.

Why does my budget only buy a few expensive clicks?

In expensive industries such as legal, insurance and finance, the top ad for a valuable keyword can cost $50 to $100 or more per click. A small budget then buys only one or two clicks, and one or two clicks rarely produce a lead. Your expected cost per lead is your cost per click divided by your conversion rate.

PPC Troubleshooter asks for your conversion rate on a slider from 0.5 to 10 leads per 100 clicks, marked Low (1), Medium (2.5) and High (5). If you don’t know your rate, use Medium. Five in 100 is already a strong result for most lead gen Search campaigns.

Worked example: a law firm pays $55 a click and has $100 to spend. $100 buys 1 click. At 2.5 leads per 100 clicks, that one click has a 2.5% chance of becoming a lead, so about 98 out of 100 firms in this spot would get nothing. One lead costs about $55 ÷ 0.025 = $2,200. Getting at least one lead in 90% of months takes 91 clicks, or $5,005 a month. Giving Smart Bidding room to work takes a daily budget of at least 3× the cost per lead, about $6,600 a day or $200,640 a month (daily × 30.4).

Why did my campaign work at first and then stop?

A drop after a good start has three usual causes: the good start was partly luck, a change restarted the learning period, or a website change broke the tracking or the form. PPC Troubleshooter tests the first cause with your numbers. It asks how many leads came in during the good stretch and since, and over how many days. If the lead rate had stayed the same, the later period’s share of all leads would follow a binomial distribution. PPC Troubleshooter reports the chance of a drop at least as big as yours. At 10% or more it calls the drop likely luck; from 2% to 10%, probably real; under 2%, real.

Worked example: 4 leads in the first 7 days, then 3 leads in the next 14 days. The weekly rate fell from 4 to 1.5, yet with the same underlying rate a split this lopsided or worse happens about 17% of the time. That drop could easily be luck, and the right move is to change nothing for two more weeks.

Why is my Google Ads cost per click going up?

Cost per click usually rises because of the bid strategy, not the market. Target impression share pays whatever it takes to reach the top of the page. Maximize conversions without a target CPA bids more as the budget grows. Broad match and AI Max reach into vaguer, pricier searches. New competitors in Auction insights and falling Quality Scores also raise prices. A higher cost per click is not a problem if cost per lead holds at or under target.

Frequently asked questions

How many clicks before I know a Google Ads campaign isn’t working?

Divide 3 by your expected conversion rate. At 2.5 conversions per 100 clicks, that is about 120 clicks. A campaign that works would get zero conversions from 119 clicks less than 5% of the time, so zero conversions past that point means something is broken. Before that point, zero conversions is often just bad luck.

How much budget do I need for Google Ads lead generation?

Your expected cost per lead is your cost per click divided by your conversion rate. For Smart Bidding, set a daily budget of at least 3 times that cost per lead, so one expensive lead can't use up the day. At a $55 cost per click and 2.5 conversions per 100 clicks, one lead costs about $2,200, so that is about $6,600 a day. A smaller budget can still bring leads with manual bids and exact match, but results will be lumpy.

Why did my Google Ads campaign work at first and then stop?

Usually one of three things. The good start was partly luck and later weeks fell back to normal. A change such as a new bid strategy, a new target or a large budget change restarted the learning period. Or a website change broke the conversion tracking or the form. With fewer than about 30 conversions in total, a drop by half can easily be luck.

Is a high cost per click a problem in Google Ads?

Not by itself. Cost per click is the price of one visitor; cost per lead is the price of a customer. If cost per click rises but cost per lead stays at or under your target, the campaign is fine. If both rise, check the bid strategy first, especially Target impression share, then broad match, competitors in Auction insights and Quality Score.
