Do 82% of law firms say Google Ads isn’t worth it? The statistic traced to its source

The claim that 82% of law firms think paid search isn’t worth the money comes from one CallRail survey taken in December 2021. The figure applied only to firms already running paid search, and almost two-thirds of the respondents worked at firms with 250 or more employees. When CallRail put the same statement to law firms in December 2024, 25% agreed. This page shows the original wording, the surveys side by side, and how the number changed as it was repeated.

By Copy for Originals · Published October 13, 2026 · Last updated October 13, 2026

Educational only, not legal advice. This page is about a marketing statistic. Copy for Originals is a Google Ads consultant, so we have a stake in whether law firms think paid search is worth it; every figure below is quoted from the report it came from.

Do 82% of law firms say Google Ads isn’t worth it?

Not in the way the figure is usually quoted. In a CallRail survey taken in December 2021, 82% of law firms that were running paid search agreed that “Paid search marketing is too expensive to get good ROI.” The figure does not cover firms that don’t advertise, it is about expense and not results, and a December 2024 CallRail survey using the same statement found 25%.

Where does the 82% figure come from?

The 82% figure comes from CallRail’s “2022 Marketing Outlook for Law Firms,” a report on a survey of 508 US law firm employees conducted in December 2021. Under the heading “Law firms that engage in paid search marketing say the following,” 82% agreed, 16% disagreed and 2% answered don’t know to the statement “Paid search marketing is too expensive to get good ROI.”

The report’s text gives the base for the companion figure: “Of those law firms who say clients find them through search, 78% say they engage in paid search marketing.” So the 78% is a share of firms whose clients find them through search, and the 82% is a share of firms that run paid search. Neither is a share of all law firms.

The short version starts in the report itself. Beside the text, a large callout reads: “78% of firms use paid search marketing, but 82% don’t think the ROI is worth it.” The callout leaves out both conditions, and it is the form most later articles copy.

CallRail sells call tracking software, and the report recommends it. That does not make the survey wrong, but it is the context the figure was published in. The report is a PDF on CallRail’s site; the paid search figures are on page 5 and the methodology is at the end.

Who answered the CallRail survey?

The respondents were 508 US-based employees of law firms with fewer than 1,000 employees, and most worked at large firms. Almost 65% worked at firms with 250 to 999 employees, 3% worked at firms with 1 to 19, and the two most common job titles were IT manager (26%) and law firm partner (16%).

A solo lawyer or a five-person firm deciding whether to try Google Ads is in the 3% row. The figure mostly describes how staff at firms of 250 people or more felt about paid search costs at the end of 2021.

The practice areas with the most respondents were civil rights law (36%) and family law (33%).

What did the survey actually ask?

The survey asked respondents whether they agreed that paid search is “too expensive to get good ROI.” It did not ask whether paid search works, whether firms track which clicks become signed cases, or whether they plan to stop. In the same survey, 59% of firms running paid search agreed they would like to spend more on it but lack the budget to compete with larger firms.

The report’s own explanation is about price: law firms “don’t think the ROI is acceptable, saying it’s too expensive.” A firm can agree that clicks are too expensive and still be getting cases from them.

Some later versions add a cause the survey never measured, such as poor tracking between clicks and signed retainers. The 2022 report does have a tracking finding, but it is a separate one: it says 82% of firms use a single-touch attribution model and 18% use multi-touch. The two 82% figures answer unrelated questions and happen to share a number.

Has the figure changed since 2021?

Yes, in both directions. CallRail’s report on its October 2022 survey says 97% of law firms that use pay-per-click find paid search too expensive to get good ROI. Its report on a December 2024 survey says 25% of law firms believe that. The samples differ in size and makeup, so the three numbers are not a trend line.

The 2025 report describes its own result this way: “For now, only 1 in 4 firms share the view that PPC is too expensive for the ROI.” In the same survey, 90% of law firms were spending on pay-per-click and 42% said they would like to spend more on paid search.

Read the three results with care. A sample of 49 is small. In the 2025 sample, 59% of respondents’ firms handled personal injury, while the 2021 sample leaned toward large firms, with civil rights and family law the most common practice areas. The 2025 figure is stated for law firms, and the two earlier ones for firms that use paid search. We did not find a 2024 edition of the report.

What the table does show is that the 82% was one reading from one sample, and that the same publisher has since reported both a higher and a much lower number.

How is the 82% figure usually quoted?

The 82% figure is usually quoted without its date, without the condition that the firms already ran paid search, and with “too expensive” changed to “doesn’t work.” The common form pairs two numbers: 78% of law firms use paid search, but 82% say the return isn’t worth it.

Each step is small. The first drops the date, the next presents the figure as current, the next changes the country, and the last changes the meaning. At least three of the four publishers sell marketing services or tracking software, as we do.

These four are examples we checked, not a count of every page that repeats the figure.

How should the 82% statistic be cited?

A citation that keeps the conditions reads: in a December 2021 CallRail survey of 508 US law firm employees, 82% of firms running paid search agreed it was “too expensive to get good ROI”; a December 2024 CallRail survey of 49 respondents put the figure at 25%.

If there is room for only one number, the more recent one is the fairer choice, with its sample size: 25% of 49 respondents in December 2024.

Three checks catch most problems with a marketing statistic like this one. Find the survey date, not the date of the article quoting it. Find the base, meaning who the percentage is a share of. Read the exact statement respondents agreed with.

Is Google Ads worth it for a law firm, then?

Google Ads is worth it for a law firm when the cost per signed case is lower than what the firm can afford to pay for a case, and no survey can answer that for a particular firm. Cost per signed case = (ad spend + agency fee) ÷ signed cases.

Our own count points the same direction as the survey without matching its number. In 312 r/LawFirm threads from October 2023 to October 2026, 188 had law firm owners describing their own paid marketing. In 63 of those (34%) the owner said they paid and got no cases or no return, and in 37 (20%) the owner said the market was too expensive for the budget. Those are forum threads, not a survey, and they cover all paid marketing, not only Google Ads. The counts are in the law firm Google Ads agency scorecard.

Whether the clicks are too expensive for a given firm is arithmetic. Cost per lead is cost per click divided by conversion rate, and the monthly budget needs to be at least three times that to tell a broken campaign from bad luck. The main guide has the table, and the Target CPA & ROAS Calculator works out what a firm can afford to pay for a lead from what a case is worth.

Limitations

  • We read CallRail’s published reports, not the survey data. The reports do not say how respondents were recruited, and only the 2026 report states a margin of error.
  • The three results come from samples of different size and makeup and from slightly different wording, so they cannot be compared as a trend.
  • The four quoting pages were read on October 13, 2026 and may have changed since. They are examples, not a full count.
  • We did not find a 2024 edition of the report. If one exists, its figure is missing from the table.
  • The r/LawFirm counts are what owners wrote in public threads, not a sample of all law firms.
  • Not legal advice.
